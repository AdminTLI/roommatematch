## LinkedIn

A cleaning roster is not the same thing as fairness.

In Dutch student houses, conflict often returns after the schoonmaakrooster is already on the fridge. The gap is usually not effort. It is the unit of measurement.

Some housemates count completed task boxes. Others count whether the shared kitchen is actually usable. A third group counts invisible noticing work: buying supplies, claiming the abandoned pan, emptying the bin before it overflows.

Preventive Law work at Zuyd (Roomietalks) and student guidance from Studiekeuzelab both point to the same early move: make cleaning, costs, guests, and communication explicit before irritation fills the silence. CBS Emancipatiemonitor 2024 also shows household labour remains socially patterned (70% of women vs 55% of men report daily household tasks), which helps explain why people arrive with different training for what “done” looks like.

Fairness is a compatibility trait. Houses that name their fairness meter in week one spare themselves a louder conversation in November.

## Instagram

Your schoonmaakrooster can be perfect and the kitchen can still feel unfair.

Not because people are lazy.
Because housemates often measure contribution differently:

✅ Task boxes completed
👀 “Is the space actually usable?”
🧠 Invisible noticing work (supplies, crumbs, the pan nobody claimed)

If one person counts hours and another counts standards, both can feel cheated while doing “their share.”

Week-one question for any student house:
What does “done” mean after dinner - and who tracks the stuff that never makes the roster?
