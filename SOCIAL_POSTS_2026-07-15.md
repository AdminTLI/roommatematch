## LinkedIn

**Student housing loneliness is not a counselling-only problem**

New Kences and CBS reporting paints a clearer picture: fewer Dutch students actually live in a rented room (44%) than say they want to (49%). Eight years ago, both numbers were higher — 52% and 59% respectively (NOS, September 2025).

CBS and NIDI research, reported by NOS this year, adds another layer. Among graduates who finished in 2023, 43% had lived at home for their entire degree, up from 31% for the 2016 cohort. Students who did move out did so later: 79% of the 2023 group still lived at home after year one, versus 63% of the 2016 group.

Kences director Jolan de Bie warns that students who stop searching for rooms still face isolation and missed social-emotional development. On Utrecht Science Park, more than 3,300 students live largely in single-room studios where neighbours pass each other without contact (NOS, 2025).

The policy question is not whether loneliness exists. It is whether housing supply, building form, and verkamering rules are measured as social infrastructure — not only as bed counts.

## Instagram

You can do everything "right" — show up to lectures, join a society, reply in the group chat — and still feel alone.

Part of that is math, not personality. Fewer students actually live on a room than want to. More finish their degree while still at their parents' place. And on some campuses, thousands of people live in studios where you rarely bump into the same neighbour twice.

Kences calls it students giving up on the room search. Residents on De Uithof call it a "social ghost neighbourhood."

Where do you feel most connected as a student — your house, your faculty, or somewhere else entirely?
